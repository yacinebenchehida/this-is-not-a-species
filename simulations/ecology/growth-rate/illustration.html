Population growth

The growth rate r

What does the growth rate r really tell us?

The growth rate per bacterium is births minus deaths, per bacterium and per unit of time. It can be positive, zero or negative. Now let food change the balance.

A steady trickle. A shared meal.

Animation in development

More mouths, less food each

Fresh food keeps arriving, but only a little at a time. As the crowd grows, each bacterium gets less and divides less often. In this model, its death rate stays constant.

Here, the carrying capacity is the population size where births balance deaths. Cells can still divide and die there: a stable total can hide a busy population.

A balance, not a stop

At low population size, food is easier to share and births exceed deaths. Near the balance point, each bacterium divides less often. Above it, deaths exceed births and the population falls back.

That feedback can produce the familiar S-shaped logistic curve. Here the supply is renewed; this is different from chapter 1’s single lunchbox.

An illustrative model

Call the actual net rate per bacterium g(N) = b(N) − d. As N increases, b(N) falls while d stays constant. For a simple straight-line example, use b(N) = b₀(1 − N/K′).

dN/dt = [b₀(1 − N/K′) − d]N
r = b₀ − d; K = K′(1 − d/b₀)
dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K)

Assume 0 < d < b₀. The straight-line birth rule applies for 0 ≤ N ≤ K′, so births never become negative. Beyond K′, births would be set to zero; the equation would no longer be exactly logistic there. K′ is where births reach zero. K is lower: it is where births equal deaths.

In this logistic equation, r is the fixed net rate at low density. The actual rate per bacterium is g(N) = r(1 − N/K), which changes with population size. The food-sharing rule is an illustration, not a detailed nutrient budget.
