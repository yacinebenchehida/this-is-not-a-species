Why does growth slow down?

With plenty of food, each cell can keep dividing at the same pace. As more cells divide, the population adds more and more new cells: that is exponential growth.

With a limited food supply, new cells leave less food available for further growth. Divisions gradually become less frequent, and the population approaches the number the available food can support: that is logistic growth.

The colours and shapes are just for fun. Every bacterium follows the same growth rules within its dish.

A little more about these dishes

The two growth rules

In the equations below, N is the population, t is time in minutes, r is the potential growth rate, and K is the number of bacteria the limited food supply can support.

Food keeps comingdN/dt = rN

One serving of fooddN/dt = rN(1 − N/K)

The division-time control sets r = ln(2) / doubling time. The food supply sets K, often called the carrying capacity.

What the illustration represents

The curves follow these equations. Each drawn character is one whole cell; completed divisions follow the times when the calculated population reaches the next whole number. At high speed, some divisions happen between screen refreshes, but every new cell is counted and shown.

The golden particles and food bar show the remaining share of the initial food supply. Particle positions do not determine which cell divides. The two dishes use the same drawing scale, which decreases to keep the growing populations visible; the bacteria are not physically shrinking.

A deliberately simple world

There are no deaths, mutations or differences in growth between colours. The model omits the food needed just to stay alive, waste products and spatial competition. It illustrates growth rules rather than a particular bacterial species or a complete laboratory culture.

Playback pauses after four model hours or when the unlimited population reaches 512 cells, whichever comes first. This viewing limit does not mean exponential growth naturally stops.
