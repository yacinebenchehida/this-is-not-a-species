Why does growth slow down?

Cells absorb nutrients and use them to grow before dividing. With an endless buffet, more cells means more cells dividing. The population doubles again and again, faster and faster: that is exponential growth.

In the lunchbox dish, growth gradually uses up the food. Each cell has less material available to grow, so it divides more slowly, and its face shows it. The population levels off at the most cells this food can make, called the carrying capacity: that is logistic growth.

The colours and shapes are just for fun. Every bacterium follows the same growth rules within its dish.

Try a little experiment

Can you get growth going again?

Let the lunchbox dish slow down, then press “Top up the lunchbox”. The same cells get a fresh supply: watch the food meter refill, the faces cheer up and growth pick up again.

Try a shorter division time too. Does it increase the final population, or simply use up the food sooner? Switch the chart to a log scale: the buffet’s curve becomes a straight line, the signature of steady doubling.

A little more about these dishes

The two growth rules

In the equations below, N is the population, t is time in minutes, r is the potential growth rate, and K is the carrying capacity: the population the available food can support.

Endless buffetdN/dt = rN

One lunchboxdN/dt = rN(1 − N/K)

The division-time control sets r = ln(2) / doubling time. In the lunchbox dish, the remaining food R decreases as new cells grow: R = K − N. The growth rate is rN × R/K, so each cell’s division speed falls as R/K falls; the faces show this share (happy above 60%, peckish above 25%, hungry below). Topping up increases R and K while leaving the existing population unchanged.

What the illustration represents

The curves follow these equations. Each drawn character is one whole cell; completed divisions follow the times when the calculated population reaches the next whole number. At high speed, some divisions happen between screen refreshes, but every new cell is counted and shown.

Each sugar cube is one unit of food, enough to make one new cell. The lunchbox dish shows exactly the food left in the model; the buffet starts with the same number of cubes and replaces every cube a cell takes. A cell that is growing towards its next division takes one cube, opens its mouth wider and wider, and swallows the whole cube just before it divides. Where the cubes lie and how cells swim to them are illustration only. With reduced motion, the pictures stay still while the food and counts update. Each dish has its own drawing scale, which decreases as its population grows but never below half size, so crowded dishes may overlap; the bacteria are not physically shrinking or growing, and the counts are always exact.

A deliberately simple world

There are no deaths, mutations or differences in growth between colours. The model omits the nutrients needed just to stay alive, waste products and spatial competition. It illustrates growth rules rather than a particular bacterial species or a complete laboratory culture.

The first view pauses after four model hours or at 512 cells in the buffet dish. Topping up the lunchbox can extend the observation, up to eight model hours or 1,024 cells. These are viewing limits; growth at the buffet would continue beyond them.
