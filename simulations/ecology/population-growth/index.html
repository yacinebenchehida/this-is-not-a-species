Why does growth slow down?

Cells absorb nutrients and use them to grow before dividing. When nutrients keep arriving, more cells means more cells dividing. The population increases faster and faster: that is exponential growth.

In the other dish, growth gradually uses up the nutrient reserve. Each cell has less material available to grow, so its division rate falls. The population approaches a plateau: that is logistic growth.

The colours and shapes are just for fun. Every bacterium follows the same growth rules within its dish.

Try a little experiment

Can you get growth going again?

Let the limited population slow down, then press “Add nutrients”. The existing cells receive a fresh supply. Watch the reserve refill and growth pick up again.

Try a shorter division time too. Does it increase the final population, or simply use up the reserve sooner? Use “Inspect the slowdown” to see the limited population’s curve more clearly.

A little more about these dishes

The two growth rules

In the equations below, N is the population, t is time in minutes, r is the potential growth rate, and K is the population supported by the available nutrients.

Nutrients replenisheddN/dt = rN

A finite nutrient reservedN/dt = rN(1 − N/K)

The division-time control sets r = ln(2) / doubling time. In the limited dish, the remaining nutrient reserve R decreases as new cells grow: R = K − N. The growth rate is rN × R/K. Adding nutrients increases R and K while leaving the existing population unchanged.

What the illustration represents

The curves follow these equations. Each drawn character is one whole cell; completed divisions follow the times when the calculated population reaches the next whole number. At high speed, some divisions happen between screen refreshes, but every new cell is counted and shown.

The golden parcels represent nutrients used to build new cells. They travel towards actual cells preparing to divide; the reserve gauge follows the model’s nutrient balance. Their size and paths are schematic, and a few feeding cells are highlighted at a time. With reduced motion, the pictures stay still while the reserve and counts update. Both dishes share one drawing scale, which decreases to keep every cell visible; the bacteria are not physically shrinking.

A deliberately simple world

There are no deaths, mutations or differences in growth between colours. The model omits the nutrients needed just to stay alive, waste products and spatial competition. It illustrates growth rules rather than a particular bacterial species or a complete laboratory culture.

The first view pauses after four model hours or at 256 cells in the replenished dish. Adding nutrients can extend the observation, up to eight model hours or 512 cells. These are viewing limits; replenished growth would continue beyond them.
