A duck's little story

Generation 0

That's genetic drift.

Alleles are versions of a gene. Neither A nor a gives a duck an advantage. Each gene copy has an equal chance of being passed on. But chance does not guarantee an equal result. In a small population, a few lucky draws can make a big difference.

Eventually, one allele can disappear altogether. The other is then fixed: every copy of this gene is the same allele. The ducks are still here; it is a version of the gene that has been lost.

How does this pond work?

The pond uses a neutral Wright–Fisher model. Its population size stays constant, and each new generation replaces the previous one. Each duck has two gene copies; every new copy is sampled independently from the previous generation's pool. A copy can be chosen more than once, or not at all.

There is no selection, mutation or migration. Once an allele is lost, it cannot return here. Starting frequencies are rounded to the nearest whole gene copy; the exact starting count is shown by the controls.

The dots are genetic labels, not plumage colours. The ducks represent individuals in a simplified population: their movements do not affect reproduction. Sexes are not modelled, and both copies can come from the same parent, so this is not a model of real duck mating.
