A duck's little story

That's genetic drift.

Every duck carries two copies of a gene: the two coloured dots on its wing. Teal and coral are different versions of that gene, called alleles. Neither gives a duck an advantage. Yet, by chance, their shares can change from one generation to the next.

Try a small pond, then a large one. Start again with the same settings. Does chance tell the same story?

A little more about this pond

Small hearts mark ducks contributing to the sampled matings. In small ponds at slow speeds, a close-up courtship follows one sampled family from each new generation; its duckling inherits one randomly chosen gene copy from each sampled parent. The nursery then fills with the complete new cohort. All N ducklings replace the N adults together, so population size stays constant. Some adults contribute to several offspring, while others contribute to none. At higher speeds, the close-up is skipped and hearts show representative sampled generations, so they remain readable. Ducks swim more gently in crowded ponds. Every individual is drawn, including in the larger populations. Choose a duck to see its dots up close.

Each generation replaces the whole population, which stays the same size. Every new gene copy is sampled independently from the previous generation's pool. A copy can be chosen more than once, or not at all. The swimming and courtship tell the story; position and appearance do not affect reproduction.

This is a neutral Wright–Fisher model, with no selection, mutation or migration. Once one allele is lost, it cannot return here; the other is then fixed. Starting frequencies are rounded to the nearest whole gene copy, with the exact count shown by the controls.

All ducks have the same yellow body. Only the two coloured dots carry genetic information. Sexes are not modelled, and both copies can come from the same parent. This is an ideal population, rather than a model of real duck mating. The family panel records that case explicitly.
