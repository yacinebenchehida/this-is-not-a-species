Same start, different stories

Many ponds at once

Every pond starts with the same teal share. Chance sends each one its own way, yet over many ponds teal ends up fixed about as often as its starting share. Try 20 ponds starting at 20% teal.

That's genetic drift.

Every duck carries two copies of a gene: the two coloured dots on its wing. Teal and coral are different versions of that gene, called alleles. Neither gives a duck an advantage. Yet, by chance, their shares can change from one generation to the next.

Not every duck becomes a parent. In each generation, chance picks some ducks several times, and the little badge over a duck counts its ducklings so far. Other ducks are never picked: at the end of the generation they turn pale, fall asleep and snore (zzz), and their gene copies are not passed on. These uneven families, decided by luck alone, are what make the colours drift.

Try a small pond, then a large one. Start again with the same settings: earlier runs stay on the chart as faint lines. Does chance tell the same story?

A little more about this pond

What the hearts mean

Each heart links the two parents of one duckling. As the heart appears, one bead flies from each parent into the nest: those are the two gene copies the duckling receives. A heart over a single duck means both copies came from that duck, which this simple model allows. Some ducks are chosen many times; others are never chosen, and doze off before the ducklings take over. At fast speeds the matings are skipped, but every generation is still counted.

At slow speeds in a small pond, one family gets a close-up first, then every other duckling is made in turn. With Watch every mating (up to 24 ducks), each pair swims to meet one at a time, and a small badge over each duck counts its ducklings so far: some ducks have several, others none. Ducks that passed on no copies fall asleep before the ducklings take over. The gene pool above the nest shows all the copies in the pond; lines link each duckling to the copies it received. Once the nest is full, the ducklings replace the adults: the number of ducks stays the same. Every duck is drawn; choose one to see its two gene copies and, after a generation has passed, its parents.

Anyone can mate with anyone

In this pond, mating is completely random: any two ducks are equally likely to become the parents of a duckling, wherever they happen to be swimming. That is why a heart sometimes links two ducks on opposite sides of the pond. Where a duck swims never affects whether it becomes a parent. Biologists call this kind of fully random mating panmixia.

The starting share matters

Here, a colour’s starting share is also its chance of eventually being the only colour left. If 75% of the gene copies start out teal, teal has a 75% chance of being the only colour left; coral has a 25% chance. Each copy still has the same chance of being passed on: teal simply starts with more copies.

These are chances, not promises for a single run. Even a rare colour can end up being the only one left. Starting at 50–50 gives both colours an equal chance. Try restarting several times with the same settings.

Bigger ponds usually take longer

With the same starting shares, chance tends to cause bigger swings from one generation to the next in a small pond. In a larger pond, the swings are usually smaller, so it generally takes more generations before only one colour remains. Individual runs can still be unusually short or long.

A larger pond changes the usual waiting time, not the chances set by the starting shares. Compare the generation counts across several runs. Swimming speed is just part of the illustration; it does not affect inheritance.

A simple population

When one allele is the only one left, it is called fixed. The missing allele cannot return here: we have not included new mutations or ducks arriving from elsewhere. This is a neutral Wright–Fisher model, meaning that all gene copies have the same chance of being passed on and each generation replaces the last at a constant population size.

All ducks have the same yellow body; only the two coloured dots carry genetic information. Sexes are not included, and both copies can sometimes come from the same parent. The family panel records that case. Position and swimming do not affect which parents are chosen.

The controls show the exact starting numbers of gene copies. Small ponds cannot represent every percentage exactly, so the starting share is rounded to the nearest whole gene copy.
